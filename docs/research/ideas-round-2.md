### Idea 13 ★ Fingerprinting synthetic tabular data in the wild

**Pitch.** Open-data repositories now host many tables that were produced by scripts or generative models, not collected from people. Round 1 hit one by accident: the 49,566-row Bangladesh road-accident dataset has independent duplicate weather columns and a death count of about 1 for every severity class. This project builds a small, interpretable test battery that flags such tables, validates it on controls where the truth is known, and applies it to Bangladeshi datasets on Mendeley and Kaggle.

**Key insight (from the pilot).** The most telling signature is *class-conditional independence*. When a generator draws each feature separately given the label, features that are correlated in real data (age, blood counts, symptoms) become independent *within each class*, while the label is still easily predictable. Real data keeps within-class structure.

**Datasets (all CC BY 4.0; I downloaded each in full, ≤2 MB except the accident file):**
- Dengue clinical, Mendeley 673swz9tb4: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/673swz9tb4 (1,018 rows)
- Dengue survey, Mendeley zdtc3n6xv2: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/zdtc3n6xv2 (1,000 rows; also on Kaggle with 4,326 downloads and 11 notebooks)
- Road accident 2007–2024, Mendeley p2kgkb5cd2: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/p2kgkb5cd2
- DiaBD https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/m8cgwxs9s6/3, DiaHealth https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/7m7555vgrn, Student Performance https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/5nvsv7ypg4, Motorbike https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/rm8t8kmhpj/3, Undergrad mental health https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/f4z2bfv7vk, USDI-30 https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/57yvy3gx8f
- Real controls: Adult, German credit and Pima diabetes from OpenML (via `sklearn.datasets.fetch_openml`).

**Pilot (what I measured).**

| Test | Real controls | Deliberately faked controls | Flagged datasets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median Cramér's V across categorical pairs | adult 0.125, credit-g 0.067, diabetes 0.252 | shuffled columns 0.000; uniform random 0.000–0.005 | road accident 0.017 |
| Share of categorical columns with near-uniform marginals | 0–14% | 100% (uniform random) | road accident 70%; dengue clinical 100% (9 columns) |
| Within-class pairs significant after Bonferroni | Pima 12/28 and 5/28; credit-g 11/190 and 27/190; adult 34/91 and 10/91 | – | **dengue clinical 1/55 and 1/55; dengue survey 0/105 and 0/120** |
| Depth-3 decision tree CV accuracy (majority rate) | Pima 0.729 (0.65); credit-g 0.728 (0.70); adult 0.809 (0.75) | – | **dengue clinical 0.985 (0.68); dengue survey 1.000 (0.53)** |

Other observations in the dengue survey (all from my own counts): AreaType splits 501/499, Gender 524/476, NS1 519/481; all 1,000 rows are in one district; Joint_Pain is missing in 491 rows. In the dengue clinical data, fever duration is spread almost evenly over 3–7 days (163–176 rows each), and platelet and WBC means differ sharply by outcome (262,944 vs 120,188 and 10,548 vs 5,079) while age does not.

**What the pilot does *not* show.** A single marginal test is weak: Student Performance (50% uniform columns), Motorbike (40%) and USDI-30 (62%) also trip it, and survey scales are legitimately near-uniform at times. My label choice for Student Performance was wrong (its target is a continuous GPA), so that row should be ignored. The within-class and separability tests are the strong ones, and they need a binary or categorical label. DiaBD looked real by these tests (within-class 40/91 and 18/91). **Treat every flag as "consistent with programmatic generation, ask the authors", never as proof.**

**Techniques.** Dependence tests (Cramér's V, mutual information, Spearman within class), uniformity and terminal-digit tests, duplicate and near-duplicate analysis, shallow-model separability, anomaly detection on test-statistic vectors across many datasets, and a meta-classifier trained on genuine vs generated tables (TVAE, CTGAN, TabDDPM-style) using the open-source `sdv` or `synthcity` packages.

**Research question and novelty.** *Can a small set of interpretable, label-aware statistics reliably separate real from generated tables, and how common are flagged tables in South Asian open-data repositories?* Related work:
- Kindji, Rojas-Barahona & Fromont (2024), *Cross-table Synthetic Tabular Data Detection*, arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13227
- Kindji, Fromont & Rojas-Barahona (2025), *Synthetic Tabular Data Detection In the Wild*, arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01937
- Kindji, Fromont & Rojas-Barahona (2025), *Robust Detection of Synthetic Tabular Data under Schema Variability*, arXiv. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00092

Those papers train supervised detectors on UCI tables and generator outputs. The gap: an interpretable, training-free audit based on conditional-independence structure, and an applied survey of a real repository. (I did not find a prior survey of this kind, but my search was limited; check before claiming it.)

**Difficulty:** Medium. **Cleaning:** Low. **Compute:** laptop.

**Risks and ethics.** (1) Naming and shaming: report flagged datasets with neutral wording, give authors a chance to respond before any public release, and consider anonymising in the paper. (2) A well-made generator that preserves within-class structure (e.g., CTGAN trained on real data) will evade the test; say so. (3) False positives on legitimately simple data (very short surveys, strongly designed questionnaires). (4) Need a large enough set of datasets for a statistical claim: plan on 60–100 tables.

**Scores:** N 5 · F 4 · D 4 · Demo 4 · P 5 (total 22)

### Idea 14 ★ An event-grounded benchmark for change-point and anomaly detectors

**Pitch.** Most time-series anomaly benchmarks have unclear or flawed ground truth (Wu & Keogh, 2021). Real series from Bangladesh contain *documented* regime events that nobody would dispute: price-floor policy changes in the stock market, the 2022 fuel crisis in the grid, the Eid exodus in traffic, the 2023 dengue surge. Use these as ground truth and compare detectors honestly: delay, false alarms, sensitivity to penalty settings.

**Datasets (all CC BY 4.0 unless noted):**
- DSE historical data (58 liquid tickers used in the pilot): https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/5mww8rb9td/1
- PGCB hourly grid data: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/vpk8spw2mm/1
- Dhaka congestion, 10-minute: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/phkvy2xwrt/1
- Daily dengue by division: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/brownsugar297/dengue-and-weather-data-bangladesh-20222025
- Event dates: floor price reintroduced July 2022 and lifted January 2024 (news, not an official gazette; confirm against SEC orders), 2022 LNG import halt, Eid-ul-Adha 27 May 2026.

**Pilot.** I computed the market-wide share of zero-return days over 58 DSE tickers, a daily shedding-energy series from PGCB, and a daily mean congestion index from the Dhaka data, then ran two standard detectors from `ruptures`.

| Series | Documented event | Detected breakpoints |
|---|---|---|
| DSE zero-return share (5-day mean) | floor price July 2022; lifted Jan 2024 | BinSeg (4 breaks): **2022-07-20**, 2022-10-11, 2023-04-26, **2024-01-21**. PELT (rbf, penalty 5): 14 breaks, including the same two |
| PGCB daily shedding (14-day mean) | fuel crisis from ~July 2022 | PELT (l2): **2022-07-24** plus 7 more |
| Dhaka daily congestion | Eid-ul-Adha ~27 May 2026 | PELT: **2026-05-26** (only one) |

Half-year zero-return share across the 58 tickers was 1–2% normally and 7.5–9.1% from H2 2022 to H1 2023, though a single stock (ACI) was frozen on 82.8% of days, so the effect is concentrated in some stocks.

**Techniques to compare.** PELT, BinSeg, BOCPD, CUSUM, matrix-profile discords, Isolation Forest on rolling features, STL residual thresholds, k-Shape cluster-switch detection. Metrics: event detection delay, precision and recall within a tolerance window, number of spurious detections per year, sensitivity to the penalty or threshold, and runtime.

**Research question and novelty.** *Which detectors recover documented regime events with the least delay and fewest false alarms across heterogeneous real series, and how much do rankings depend on tolerance and tuning?* Related work:
- Wu & Keogh (2021), *Current Time Series Anomaly Detection Benchmarks are Flawed and are Creating the Illusion of Progress*, IEEE TKDE. https://doi.org/10.1109/TKDE.2021.3112126
- Yeh et al. (2016), *Matrix Profile I*, ICDM. https://doi.org/10.1109/ICDM.2016.0179
- Killick, Fearnhead & Eckley (2012), PELT, JASA. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2012.737745

The gap: an openly licensed benchmark with externally documented events from a non-Western setting. (I did not exhaustively search existing event-labelled benchmarks, so check before claiming priority.)

**Difficulty:** Easy–Medium. **Cleaning:** Low–Medium. **Compute:** laptop.

**Risks.** Only about 6–8 events, so statistics are thin; mitigate by adding events (COVID lockdown in traffic or grid data, the 2024 floods in WFP prices) and by bootstrapping over windows. Event dates need citations, not memory. Several of these series are the same ones used in round 1, so avoid double-using them if you also pick idea 1 or 8.

**Scores:** N 4 · F 5 · D 4 · Demo 4 · P 4 (total 21)

### Idea 15 ★ The Bangladesh legal code as a graph

**Pitch.** The Bangladesh Laws portal data is public, and a recent dataset packs 1,484 acts with titles, sections, footnotes and a repealed flag. Nobody (as far as I found) has built a citation and repeal network over it. Questions: which acts are the legal hubs, how do citation clusters line up with legal areas and eras (colonial, Pakistan, post-1971), and which *live* acts still refer to *repealed* ones (stale references a legislator would want to fix)?

**Dataset.** Bangladesh Legal Acts Dataset (Adib Sakhawat), CC BY 4.0, single 61.9 MB JSON: https://huggingface.co/datasets/sakhadib/Bangladesh-Legal-Acts-Dataset (also Kaggle). Source portal: Bangladesh Laws (bdlaws).

**Pilot.** I matched title-like strings (e.g., "The Customs Act, 1969") in section text and footnotes against the corpus titles.

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Acts in file | 1,484 (582 English, 698 Bengali, 203 unknown language) |
| Unique normalised titles | 721 |
| Flagged repealed | 203 |
| Distinct title-like mentions found | 3,771 |
| Matched citation edges | 1,336 (599 citing acts, 237 cited acts) |
| Live→repealed edges | 324 (239 distinct live citing acts) |
| Most cited | Bangladesh Bank (Demonetisation of Currency Notes) Order, 1972 (170 in-edges); Bangladesh Chartered Accountants Order, 1973 (117); Customs Act, 1969 (85); Public Demands Recovery Act, 1913 (83) |

**Caveats from the pilot.** The 170-edge count for a 1972 currency order is probably an artefact (repeated amendment footnotes or a template phrase), not genuine legal centrality. Only English text is covered, so about half the corpus is not yet in the graph. The repealed flag comes from the portal and is unverified. Title matching is crude; a proper entity-linking step is the main technical work and a good research component.

**Techniques.** Citation extraction and entity linking (fuzzy title matching, year and act-number features), directed graph analysis (PageRank, HITS, in/out-degree, k-core), community detection (Louvain) compared with the legal subject areas and era, temporal network growth, stale-reference detection, link prediction (which acts *should* cite each other), and text-similarity clustering of sections (TF-IDF, embeddings). Bengali half: Bangla numeral normalisation and the same linking approach.

**Research question and novelty.** *What is the citation and repeal structure of Bangladesh's statute book, and how many live statutes depend on repealed ones?* Related work:
- Mandal, Nath & Mizan et al. (2017), *A visual search engine for Bangladeshi laws*, arXiv (Doc2Vec layout, citation links, pilot with 15 users; not a network analysis study). https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05233
- Koniaris, Anagnostopoulos & Vassiliou (2015), *Network Analysis in the Legal Domain: A complex model for European Union legal sources*, arXiv (method reference; found a power-law structure). https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.05237

I found no graph-mining study of Bangladeshi statute citations, though my search was a single query.

**Difficulty:** Medium–Hard. **Cleaning:** High (entity linking, Bengali text). **Compute:** laptop (62 MB).

**Risks.** Legal accuracy: you are not a lawyer, so frame findings as "candidate stale references for expert review". The repealed flag and act text can themselves be wrong. Bengali coverage may take most of the effort. A law-informatics venue may want a legal collaborator.

**Scores:** N 4 · F 3 · D 3 · Demo 5 · P 4 (total 19)

### Idea 16 Scouting the field: a topic-saturation map of Bangladeshi computer-science research

**Pitch.** Use OpenAlex to mine every Bangladesh-affiliated computer-science work from 2015 to 2025, with abstracts, venues, citations and co-authors. Build a topic × application-domain × data-modality map and a "saturation score" (how many papers per topic and dataset, and how fast it is growing), to show which areas are crowded and which are open. It also doubles as a way to pick a project.

**Data.** OpenAlex API (documented in Priem, Piwowar & Orr 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01833). I believe the data is CC0 but did not verify the licence page.

**Pilot (API counts I pulled).** 37,198 works, 2015–2025, with a Bangladesh affiliation and field = Computer Science. By year: 1,143 (2015), 2,769 (2020), 7,984 (2025). Top subfields in 2015: Computer Networks and Communications (181), Artificial Intelligence (134); in 2025: Artificial Intelligence (1,627), Computer Vision (868), Information Systems (849), with Plant Science (489) and Health Information Management (405) appearing in the top six. 2020–2025 top venues: International Conference on Computer and Information Technology (1,823 works), arXiv (1,141), SSRN (1,113), IEEE Access (460). Co-author countries after Bangladesh: United States (3,345), India (1,863), China (1,692), Australia (1,514). 23,271 works for 2020–2025 have abstracts.

**Techniques.** NMF/BERTopic topic models, burst and trend detection, keyword co-occurrence and collaboration networks, citation-based diffusion, density estimation over topic × domain grids to find empty cells, association rules between datasets and tasks.

**Research question and novelty.** *Where is Bangladeshi CS research saturated or sparse, and how has the topic mix shifted?* Related work: bibliometric studies exist for library science (https://publications.drdo.gov.in/ojs/index.php/djlit/article/view/16854) and Dhaka University via Scopus, but my search found none for Bangladeshi CS using OpenAlex with topic evolution. Treat that as "not found", not "does not exist".

**Difficulty:** Easy–Medium. **Cleaning:** Medium. **Compute:** laptop.

**Risks.** Affiliation metadata is noisy; abstract coverage is partial; topic models need careful validation. Scientometrics reviewers may expect established bibliometric methods.

**Scores:** N 3 · F 5 · D 4 · Demo 5 · P 3 (total 20)

### Idea 17 Rule explosion on small surveys: how to get a few valid, readable rules

**Pitch.** Student papers often run Apriori on a survey and report a handful of rules. The pilot shows the real problem on small surveys is not false discoveries but an explosion of near-duplicate rules. This project compares ways of shrinking the rule set: statistical filters (Fisher + FDR/FWER, permutation nulls), closed and non-redundant rules, and compression-based summaries (Krimp/MDL), judged by size, information retained and a small human study.

**Data.** USDI-30 depression survey (823 × 37, https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/57yvy3gx8f) and undergraduate mental health survey (501 × 63, https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/f4z2bfv7vk), both CC BY 4.0, plus DiaBD or the motorbike survey as extras.

**Pilot.** Rules of length ≤3 from FP-Growth (`mlxtend`), compared with the mean over shuffled datasets in which each column was permuted independently (5–10 shuffles):

| Dataset, thresholds | Rules (real) | Rules (shuffled null, mean) | Passing Fisher + BH, q=0.05 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mental health, sup ≥0.15, conf ≥0.8, lift ≥1.2 | 11,418 | 1.1 | 11,418 |
| USDI-30, sup ≥0.10, conf ≥0.7, lift ≥1.2 | 2,890 | 0.2 | 2,890 |
| USDI-30, sup ≥0.05, conf ≥0.6, lift ≥1.15 | 20,387 | 113.4 (≈1%) | 20,382 |
| USDI-30, sup ≥0.03, conf ≥0.5, lift ≥1.1 | 64,082 | 5,044.6 (≈8%) | 61,105 |

So the starting hypothesis (many rules are spurious) failed. The interesting question is how to reduce 11k–64k mostly redundant, true rules to something a person can read. Note the Fisher + BH step here is applied after mining on the same data, so it is optimistic; a proper study would use holdout or the permutation-based correction.

**Related work.** Hämäläinen & Webb (2018), *A tutorial on statistically sound pattern discovery*, DMKD. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-018-0590-x · Vreeken, van Leeuwen & Siebes (2011), *Krimp: mining itemsets that compress*, DMKD. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10618-010-0202-x

**Difficulty:** Medium. **Compute:** laptop (the 63-column run took about a minute; 10 shuffles were slower).

**Risks.** The methods are known; the novelty is the systematic comparison and a human-readability measure on small surveys, so expect a workshop-level contribution. A human study needs participants.

**Scores:** N 3 · F 4 · D 4 · Demo 3 · P 3 (total 17)

### Idea 18 Duplicate and leakage audit of public Bangla text datasets

**Pitch.** Many Bangla classification papers train on public Mendeley or Kaggle datasets. If the same text appears in train and test, scores are inflated. Audit how many duplicates exist inside and across datasets, and how much they matter for different model types.

**Data (Mendeley, all CC BY 4.0):** BECS https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/93r2cxvbdx (15,584 reviews) · RevBangla https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/bnbbcdsf4m · BanglaEcomReviewCorpus https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/kkzfrvhbhp · Bangla multilabel cyberbully https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/sz5558wrd4/3.

**Pilot.** After Unicode normalisation, Bangla-digit mapping and punctuation removal:

| Dataset | Rows | % rows in exact-duplicate groups | % in normalised-duplicate groups | Near-duplicate (char-n-gram cosine ≥0.9) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BECS | 15,385 | 21.2 | 22.9 | 20.8% (74 duplicate texts carry conflicting labels) |
| RevBangla | 4,999 | 0.04 | 4.1 | 12.8% |
| BanglaEcomReviewCorpus | 8,685 | 0.0 | 0.4 | 8.7% |
| Cyberbully | 12,175 | 28.5 | 31.5 | 19.7% |

Cross-dataset exact overlaps were small (4–146 texts per pair; for example BECS × cyberbully 146, with 9.8% of the smaller set having a near-duplicate in the larger one). **Effect on accuracy** (TF-IDF char n-grams + logistic regression, 5 random splits): on BECS, 21.2% of test rows had an exact twin in train; accuracy was 0.693 with a random split versus 0.681 when duplicates were grouped (1.1 points); on RevBangla the difference was −0.2 points and on BanglaEcomReviewCorpus −0.9 points.

**Honest reading.** Duplicates are common but mostly short generic comments, and a linear model barely benefits. The inflation may be larger for fine-tuned transformers, which could memorise; testing that needs Colab GPU time and is not done here.

**Related work.** Elangovan, He & Verspoor (2021), *Memorization vs. Generalization: Quantifying Data Leakage in NLP Performance Evaluation*, EACL. https://aclanthology.org/2021.eacl-main.113 · Search results also showed 2026 Bangla preprints that touch on contamination and duplicates, for example MathShikkha, which runs exact and near-duplicate checks on a Bangla math set (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08503); I only read search snippets, so check these before claiming novelty.

**Difficulty:** Easy–Medium. **Compute:** laptop for the audit; Colab for transformer fine-tuning.

**Scores:** N 3 · F 4 · D 4 · Demo 3 · P 3 (total 17)

### Idea 19 Dependency-structure shift in Bangladesh's power grid

**Pitch.** Detect *how the relationships between variables change* at a regime break, and not just that a level changed. Pilot: day-to-day changes in liquid-fuel generation barely related to changes in load shedding before the crisis, and strongly after. This idea extends it to a full causal or dependency graph per regime (PCMCI, Granger, graphical lasso) and tests whether the graphs differ more than chance.

**Data.** PGCB hourly grid data: https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/vpk8spw2mm/1 (CC BY 4.0).

**Pilot.** Spearman correlations of daily changes. Pre-crisis (2018–2021, 1,461 days) versus crisis (Jul 2022–2024, 915 days): liquid fuel × load shedding 0.01 → **0.43**; liquid fuel × coal 0.18 → 0.29; liquid fuel × imports 0.32 → 0.26; gas × anything stayed near 0 (≤0.24). The lagged check (gas change today vs liquid-fuel change tomorrow) was weak in both periods (0.10 and 0.04).

**Related work.** Runge et al. (2019), *Detecting and quantifying causal associations in large nonlinear time series datasets* (PCMCI), Science Advances. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aau4996

**Risks.** Overlaps with idea 1 from round 1 (same data); pick one. Causal claims are fragile; present as "dependency structure".

**Scores:** N 3 · F 4 · D 4 · Demo 3 · P 3 (total 17)

### 5.1 Idea 13: Fingerprinting synthetic tabular data

**Baselines vs the interesting method**

| Task | Baseline | Interesting method |
|---|---|---|
| Is a table generated? | Duplicate rows, uniform marginals | Label-aware battery: within-class dependence, separability by shallow trees, cross-column consistency checks |
| Combining signals | Eyeball | Standardise each statistic against a reference set of real tables (z-scores), then a small interpretable score (logistic regression or a rule list) |
| Generalisation | Train a detector on one table | Leave-one-table-out evaluation across 30+ real UCI/OpenML tables and generated copies |
| Repository survey | Read a few datasets | Run the battery on all small tabular datasets from a defined Mendeley/Kaggle sample (e.g., 60–100 tables); report prevalence with confidence intervals |
